Declarative Dragging

Joshua Horowitz, Andrew Blinn, Jeffrey Heer

★ Best Paper Award, UIST 2026
paper video explorable supplement demos

What if making a diagram draggable was as simple as saying what it can become?

Drag-and-drop makes the digital graspable. But designing bespoke drag interactions involves a lot of fiddly code to handle geometry, feedback, and animations. As a result, many people, from educators to game designers, mathematicians to creative tool builders, often don't bother.

Declarative dragging inverts the problem. Rather than programming all that visual behavior directly, you simply say, for each element you want to make draggable, which future states of your application dragging it should lead to. Our system speculatively renders those states to work out where things can go, then combines these possible worlds into a smooth, responsive drag-and-drop interaction.

Our goal with this project is to let people who think in abstractions keep working within the semantic worlds they inhabit, while helping them turn the rules of their worlds into rich tactile interactions in ours.

How it works

D2 is model-driven: An interface author makes an element draggable by specifying which future model states should be accessible by dragging it. An engine speculatively renders these states to view trees, extracts the position of the dragged element in each of these renderings, and uses these to synthesize a drag-and-drop interaction, all without detailed work required by the interface author.

To provide greater expressive range and customizability, D2 is composable: simple model-driven drag-and-drop behaviors can be combined to create more complex ones, and modifiers can layer features like floating, snapping, and transitions. For more details, see §3.2 and §5 of the paper.

Where it came from

Declarative dragging is actually a fairly personal project for both Josh and I. For myself, the idea of a math game based on tangible expressions has been a long time obsession. I made a few prototypes over the years, but none proved juicy enough to satisfy. Back in 2022, I came up with the scheme to make it draggable, and excitedly sketched out a primitive version of what would become model-driven dragging. However, implementing it seemed daunting, so it sat on the whiteboard for the next two years, mocking me every time I went to make a sandwich. — Andrew
In 2023, Andrew visited Seattle. Toward the end of a lovely walk, we stopped for coffee. Andrew showed me Nool [a math game prototype], and told me about his idea to make it draggable. I was intrigued, but I didn't do anything about it until last year. That's when my friend Elliot was playing with order-preserving maps between trees. I came up with a fun way to draw them, but I realized I didn't just want to draw them – I wanted to reach in and pull them around, and feel out the mathematical space they determined. That felt pretty tough, until I remembered Andrew's ideas. And over a day or two I whipped up something that worked. It felt very exciting to turn these static diagrams into something alive, and it was exciting to imagine what else might be possible. So I kept building demos, and iterating on the ideas, until it was clear that you could really do a lot with it, and I brought Andrew back in, and we built a system, and wrote a paper. — Josh
